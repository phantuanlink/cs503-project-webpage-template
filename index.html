V-JEPA-BEV: Multi-Task BEV Perception with Frozen Self-Supervised Video Features

Masa Duric, Nathan Phan Tuan Linh, Paul Bourgois, Tancrède Lamort de Gail
EPFL CS-503 Visual Intelligence · Spring 2026
Report Video Project Code nuScenes

V-JEPA-BEV asks a simple question: for multi-task BEV perception, does temporal video understanding (V-JEPA 2.1) help more than strong spatial understanding (DINOv3) when the backbone is frozen?

Abstract

Bird's-Eye-View (BEV) is the dominant scene representation in autonomous driving, providing a unified top-down framework for detection, segmentation, tracking, and planning from a single shared feature map. Its quality is fundamentally bottlenecked by the image backbone — but it has been unclear what kind of backbone matters most: one that understands video dynamics, or one with strong static spatial grounding.

We propose V-JEPA-BEV: a BEV pipeline driven by a frozen self-supervised backbone, paired with a multi-scale convolutional adapter (gated residual blocks across feature scales) and BEVFormer's deformable cross-attention. We compare two frozen backbones head-to-head under a matched-compute setup — V-JEPA 2.1, which excels at temporal consistency, and DINOv3, which excels at spatial grounding — across four downstream tasks: 3D object detection, HD map segmentation, instance tracking, and ego-trajectory prediction.

On nuScenes, our best variant matches or improves on the matched-compute BEVFormer baseline across [TODO: which metrics], with simultaneous gains across multiple tasks. The headline finding, however, is comparative: spatial understanding in the encoder appears to matter more than temporal understanding for the BEV-projection problem. Encoding dynamics in the backbone is less useful than we expected, suggesting that dynamics should be handled downstream rather than baked into the image encoder.

I. Introduction

Autonomous driving systems rely on accurate perception of the surrounding environment, and Bird's-Eye-View (BEV) has emerged as the dominant scene representation because it provides a unified top-down spatial framework for multiple downstream tasks. A single shared BEV feature map can drive detection, segmentation, tracking, and motion prediction simultaneously, enabling joint reasoning across multi-camera inputs without redundant per-task encoders.

The quality of this representation is fundamentally bottlenecked by the image backbone. BEVFormer v2 observes that BEV supervision provides only an indirect gradient to the backbone — the task loss travels backward through multiple transformer layers before reaching backbone weights, giving insufficient signal for a general 2D backbone to learn 3D scene properties. The resulting BEV features are spatially noisy, temporally inconsistent, and biased toward whichever downstream task dominates the loss, degrading every task that shares the representation.

Because the BEV feature map is shared across all tasks, a weak backbone hurts everything simultaneously, which makes fixing the backbone a higher-leverage intervention than improving any individual task head. Existing solutions either require expensive depth pre-training or an auxiliary perspective detection head, adding significant complexity. We instead ask whether a strong frozen self-supervised backbone is sufficient — and, more interestingly, what kind of pre-training matters.

Driving is a deeply dynamic setting: ego motion, other agents, occlusions, and lighting all change frame to frame. It is tempting to assume that a backbone that understands video dynamics should beat one trained only on still images. Yann LeCun has made the broader case explicitly:

"A 17-year-old can learn to drive in 20 hours. Why can't we train an AI to do that? Because we are missing something big: World Models. A teenager understands the consequences of their actions in the real world."

This motivates our central question:

Does a backbone that understands temporal dynamics (V-JEPA 2.1) help BEV perception more than one with stronger spatial grounding (DINOv3), when both are frozen?

To answer it we build V-JEPA-BEV: a BEV pipeline with a frozen image backbone, a multi-scale convolutional adapter (with gated residual blocks bridging different feature scales), and the standard BEVFormer transformer with deformable cross-attention and temporal self-attention. We swap the backbone (V-JEPA 2.1 vs DINOv3) and train identical downstream stacks on four nuScenes tasks. Our contributions are: (i) a careful matched-compute backbone comparison isolating temporal vs spatial pre-training for BEV, (ii) a multi-scale adapter that absorbs the resolution mismatch between modern ViT backbones and standard BEVFormer features, and (iii) a simultaneous evaluation across detection, segmentation, tracking, and trajectory prediction.

II. Related Works

Multi-camera BEV perception

BEVFormer establishes the standard multi-camera BEV pipeline by lifting per-view image features into a shared BEV feature map through spatial cross-attention with learnable BEV queries, then refining across time with a deformable temporal self-attention layer. Most subsequent BEV systems retain this template and vary the backbone, the projection geometry, or the task heads. We adopt BEVFormer's transformer and deformable-attention skeleton unchanged, and intervene only at the backbone.

The backbone-supervision gap

BEVFormer v2 identifies that BEV supervision provides insufficient gradient to adapt a generic image backbone to 3D scene properties, and addresses this by adding a perspective detection head that supervises the backbone directly in 2D. They report a persistent ≈3% NDS gap that does not close with extended training. Our approach is complementary: rather than adding auxiliary supervision to train the backbone, we use a frozen backbone whose features are already spatially and temporally grounded.

Generative refinement of BEV features

BEVDiffuser takes the orthogonal approach of leaving the backbone alone and refining the BEV features themselves with a training-time diffusion denoiser conditioned on ground-truth object layouts, reporting +12.3% mAP on detection. Their refiner is discarded at inference. We adopt this teacher–student framing for our stretch goal but replace diffusion with flow matching, and extend the evaluation from detection-only to all four tasks.

Self-supervised representations: temporal and spatial

V-JEPA 2.1 learns dense, temporally consistent patch features from internet video without labels, using a context-prediction objective in feature space. The 2.1 release reports 47.9 mIoU on ADE20K semantic segmentation under linear probing (vs. 22.2 for V-JEPA 1.0), evidence that the encoder learns spatially meaningful features rather than only sequence-level summaries. Its core asset for our setting is temporal consistency: features for the same physical content stay stable across consecutive frames of a clip.

DINOv3 is the natural counterpart on the image-SSL side: a frozen ViT trained on a large image corpus with a self-distillation objective that produces unusually clean dense patch features. DINOv3 carries no temporal signal but provides extremely strong spatial grounding — the property that BEVFormer v2's perspective head was added to recover. The two backbones therefore put our research question — temporal vs spatial — in a single controlled comparison, with all other components (adapter, BEV transformer, task heads, training schedule) held fixed.

To our knowledge, neither backbone has been applied to multi-camera BEV perception in a controlled head-to-head, and the conclusion of that comparison is the main empirical contribution of this work.

III. Methodology

3.1 Pipeline overview

Figure 1 summarises the full pipeline. Six surround-camera views are encoded independently by a frozen ViT backbone — either V-JEPA 2.1 (temporal) or DINOv3 (spatial). A multi-scale convolutional adapter projects the resulting tokens into the BEV transformer's working dimension while bridging the backbone's coarse patch grid to multiple finer feature scales. The BEV transformer then lifts these multi-scale, multi-view tokens into a shared BEV feature map $X^{\mathrm{BEV}} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$ using deformable spatial cross-attention and BEVFormer-style temporal self-attention. Four task heads read from this shared feature map: 3D object detection, HD map segmentation, per-agent motion prediction, and ego-trajectory prediction.

Figure 1. The V-JEPA-BEV pipeline. A frozen ViT-B backbone (V-JEPA 2.1 or DINOv3) encodes each camera; a multi-scale convolutional adapter with gated residual blocks bridges backbone tokens to BEVFormer-style feature maps; the BEV transformer fuses them into a shared BEV map via deformable spatial cross-attention (8 / 12 / 16 sampling points across three scales) and temporal self-attention; four task heads read from the same map.

3.2 Frozen backbones: V-JEPA 2.1 and DINOv3

We compare two frozen ViT backbones. V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-B ingests a short clip per camera and produces $T$ temporal slices of $14 \times 24$ patch tokens at $d_v = 768$, giving a cached tensor of shape $[B, 6, T \cdot 14 \cdot 24, d_v]$. Its strength is temporal consistency of dense patch features. DINOv3 ViT-B ingests a single frame per camera and produces a denser patch grid of the same channel width, with the opposite trade-off: no native temporal signal but markedly stronger spatial grounding.

In both cases we keep the backbone frozen end-to-end. Freezing has three motivations:

Scientific. The point of the project is to test whether off-the-shelf SSL features are sufficient, and whether temporal or spatial pre-training matters more. Fine-tuning would conflate "good SSL representation" with "well-adapted fine-tuned representation" and would also entangle the two backbones with the optimizer's gradient flow.
Computational. A frozen backbone lets us precompute and cache features once per scene and reuse them across all training runs and ablations, turning the per-run cost into BEV-transformer training time.
Methodological. Frozen features that already work are stronger evidence of representation quality than features that are coerced into working by gradient flow.

3.3 Multi-scale convolutional adapter

Both backbones produce tokens at a much coarser spatial resolution than BEVFormer's deformable cross-attention expects, and the input image resolutions we use (896×1600 full, downsampled to 25%, 17.6%, and ~6% of input area at successive scales) need to map cleanly onto a multi-scale feature pyramid. We bridge this with a multi-scale convolutional adapter.

The adapter has a shared linear adapter that projects the backbone's ViT-B channels (768) down to BEVFormer's working dimension (256), followed by a small pyramid of convolutional refinement blocks at successively finer spatial scales. Each scale applies a stack of plain Conv + Residual blocks, plus a gated convolutional block that interpolates between the scale's own features and the upsampled features from the previous scale, with a learned sigmoid gate selecting which to trust per token. The pyramid produces feature maps at multiple resolutions, which the BEV transformer's spatial cross-attention then samples from with progressively more sampling points (Section 3.4).

The adapter is the only place where the two backbones differ at the architecture level — V-JEPA features are first temporally reduced (last-slice, mean, or token-wise gated fusion of the current and previous V-JEPA slices) before entering the adapter; DINOv3 features are single-frame and skip this step. Everything downstream is identical, which lets the table-level comparison cleanly isolate the choice of backbone.

3.4 BEV transformer with deformable attention

The BEV transformer follows BEVFormer's design. Learnable BEV queries arranged on a $H \times W$ grid (default 200×200) interact with adapter features through two attention mechanisms per encoder layer.

Spatial cross-attention projects each BEV query's 3D pillar into every camera via known intrinsics and extrinsics, producing a small set of reference points per query–camera pair. Deformable attention then refines around each reference point: a linear layer on the query predicts $H \times L \times P$ sampling offsets and matching attention weights, and bilinear sampling fetches values at those offset locations. This keeps spatial cross-attention $O(N_{\text{query}} \cdot H \cdot P)$ rather than $O(N_{\text{query}} \cdot N_{\text{tokens}})$, and crucially seeds the layer with a geometrically valid starting point rather than asking it to discover 3D-to-2D correspondences from scratch.

We use the multi-scale feature pyramid from the adapter (Section 3.3) and allocate 8, 12, and 16 sampling points per query at the three pyramid scales respectively, giving more samples on the finer-resolution maps where local image detail matters most. This is tuned for the matched 224×384 / 386×656 / 448×800 BEV-projection setup we use in the main experiments.

Temporal self-attention fuses the current BEV queries with the previous frame's BEV feature map warped into the current ego frame via known pose. Dynamic objects are intentionally not warped — the layer learns to integrate their inconsistent positions into a motion-aware representation. Crucially, this is a BEV-level temporal mechanism: it propagates state across BEVFormer frames regardless of which backbone was used. Our best variant ("V-JEPA-BEV with temporal attention") includes both V-JEPA's intra-clip temporal fusion (in the adapter) and BEVFormer's inter-frame temporal self-attention.

3.5 Shared BEV, four task heads

All four task heads consume the same BEV feature map $X^{\mathrm{BEV}}$. Heads are independently registered modules, and the detector instantiates them conditionally based on its config, so any subset can be enabled without touching code. Three of the four heads are designed to be lightweight, isolating the headline question — whether the backbone is the bottleneck — from confounders introduced by heavy task-specific decoders.

3D object detection. A DETR3D-style decoder with 900 object queries, six decoder layers, and Hungarian matching. Predicts 3D box parameters, class scores, and velocity.
HD map segmentation. A three-layer convolutional decoder (256 → 128 → 64 → $C$) producing per-pixel sigmoid logits for six nuScenes map classes (drivable_area, ped_crossing, walkway, stop_line, carpark_area, divider). Loss is sigmoid focal ($\gamma=2$, $\alpha=0.25$). No upsampling — the BEV grid is already at the target resolution.
Per-agent motion prediction. A shared MLP on the 900 detection-query features produces six future waypoints per query in each agent's local frame. Supervision is applied only on Hungarian-matched positives and only on moving agents (speed > 0.5 m/s), which prevents the stationary majority from trivially driving the loss to zero.
Ego trajectory. A single learnable ego query, offset-initialised by an 18-D CAN-bus prior (speed, yaw, acceleration), cross-attends over the BEV map through two decoder layers and predicts six future ego positions via a small MLP.

3.6 Matched-compute training setup

The headline comparison between V-JEPA-BEV and DINOv3-BEV is meaningful only if the two pipelines are matched on everything but the backbone. We use identical adapter architecture, identical BEV transformer hyperparameters (number of layers, BEV resolution, embedding dimension, sampling-point counts), identical task heads, identical loss weights, identical optimiser and schedule, and the same number of training epochs. The two backbones are both ViT-B, share the same hidden dimension ($d = 768$), and are both fully frozen. The only architectural difference is that V-JEPA features pass through an additional temporal-fusion step (last / mean / gated) before the adapter, since they arrive as multiple temporal slices; DINOv3 features are single-frame and skip this step.

This setup means any observed performance gap reflects the backbone's pre-training objective — temporal video SSL vs. spatial image SSL — rather than a confounded mix of architecture, supervision, and tuning differences. That property is what makes the takeaway in Section 5 interpretable.

IV. Results

4.1 Experimental setup

We evaluate on the nuScenes dataset (1000 driving scenes, six surround cameras at 1600×900, 20-second clips with 2 Hz keyframe annotations). All training and validation use the standard nuScenes splits. We report mAP and NDS for 3D detection, mIoU for HD map segmentation, AMOTA for tracking, and ADE / FDE for both per-agent motion and ego-trajectory prediction. Training is performed on 4×NVIDIA V100 GPUs (EPFL Izar cluster) with BEV resolution 200×200 and embedding dimension 256. V. Conclusion and Limitations

5.1 Key takeaway: spatial > temporal in the encoder

We set out to test whether a frozen video backbone — V-JEPA 2.1 — would beat a frozen image backbone — DINOv3 — for multi-task BEV perception, on the intuition that driving is dynamic and a backbone that understands video should win. Our experiments suggest the opposite. Temporal understanding in the encoder is not as important as spatial understanding. DINOv3's stronger spatial grounding translates more directly into the BEV-projection task; V-JEPA's temporal consistency does help, but the gain is recovered almost as well by adding a single temporal self-attention layer at the BEV level rather than baking dynamics into the backbone.

We read this as a comment on where the bottleneck of BEV perception actually sits: lifting six 2D camera views into a single 3D BEV grid is a fundamentally spatial task. Backbones that produce cleaner per-pixel patch features make this lifting easier; backbones that produce temporally consistent but spatially coarser features do not. Dynamics in driving may be better encoded directly downstream — at the BEV level, in the tracker, or in a dedicated representation such as a driving tokenizer ("DriveTok") — rather than inside the image encoder itself.

5.2 Limitations

Domain gap. Neither V-JEPA 2.1 nor DINOv3 is pre-trained on driving footage. Some of the gap we observe relative to BEVFormer-tiny on [TODO: which metric] may reflect this; a driving-specific SSL backbone would likely close it further on both sides.
Frozen-only regime. We deliberately restrict to frozen backbones to isolate representation quality from backbone adaptation. BEV projection is, however, genuinely hard, and partial fine-tuning of the backbone is a plausible way to recover the remaining gap — we leave that to future work.
Multi-task interactions. Individual task losses compete during training. We mitigate this by adding tasks incrementally and tuning per-head loss weights manually; a principled multi-task balancing scheme is left to future work.
Single dataset. Our evaluation is limited to nuScenes. Cross-dataset transfer to Waymo or Argoverse would test how much of the gain is dataset-specific.
Compute. Training on 4×V100 fits the project budget but constrains the ablation surface. A larger backbone (ViT-L), additional auxiliary losses, or extended hyperparameter sweeps would require more compute than this setup supports.

5.3 Future work

Three directions follow naturally from the takeaway above.

Push frozen further, then start fine-tuning. Our results suggest frozen SSL backbones are promising for BEV perception. The natural next step is partial fine-tuning of the last few backbone blocks — BEV projection is a difficult 3D-spatial task that may benefit from a small amount of backbone adaptation while still preserving the SSL representation.
Encode dynamics downstream, not in the encoder. Given that temporal pre-training is less useful than spatial pre-training in the encoder, the natural place to inject dynamics is downstream — either at the BEV level (richer temporal attention, recurrence, or trajectory queries) or in a separate driving-token representation that the BEV map can consult.
Auxiliary signals for a generalist AV model. A BEV map alone may not be enough for a generalist autonomous-driving system. Auxiliary signals derived from the encoder but adjacent to BEV — depth, optical flow, scene classification, semantic segmentation — could complement the shared BEV representation without forcing every task through it.
